# What Makes Presentations Great (and Bad): A Synthesized, Cited Reference

A working reference for designing an actual slideshow deck. Every substantive claim
is tied to a primary or primary-adjacent source (listed at the bottom). Folk-wisdom
claims that turned out to be myths are flagged explicitly in their own section.

---

## 0. House rules (Jo's principles — these take precedence)

These are the non-negotiables for *this* deck. Each is reinforced by the research cited below — noted in brackets.

- **A presentation is a story told through panels.** Whether it's the story of a stock's health over six months or the story of a kid who beat cancer, the deck is a narrative, not a report. Every panel is a beat in that story. [Reinforced by Duarte's Sparkline and the TED throughline — §1, §2]
- **The audience must feel they went on a journey — and that they're smarter at the end of it.** Engagement is the whole game. They should leave with something they didn't have walking in, and feel the arc of having earned it. [Reinforced by the Big Idea + "what is → what could be" contrast, and the one-repeatable-idea rule — §1, §2]
- **Never fill a panel with small text — especially long bullet lists.** That's not what a panel is *for*. A slide is a visual support, not a document. If it needs a paragraph, it belongs in the talk or a handout, not the panel. [Reinforced by the redundancy effect and slides-as-support, not teleprompter — §3, §4]
- **Don't overuse "interesting" transitions — it makes everyone mad.** Flashy slide transitions and gratuitous motion read as amateur and distract from the story. Restraint. [Reinforced by Mayer's coherence principle — irrelevant motion competes for working memory — §4]

## 1. What great presentations share

The repeatable qualities that show up across the most credible presentation/communication literature:

- **One central idea, stated and repeatable.** TED's house rule is that every talk needs a single "throughline" — a connecting theme — captured in roughly fifteen words or less, and the audience should be able to repeat the core idea after hearing you. Duarte frames the same thing as the **Big Idea**: your point of view *plus* what's at stake for the audience. (TED Guide to Public Speaking; Duarte) [1][9]

- **Contrast and tension drive engagement.** Duarte's analysis of hundreds of great talks found the strongest ones oscillate between "what is" (the status quo / problem) and "what could be" (the better future). This back-and-forth — her **Sparkline** — creates the tension that holds attention and motivates action. (Duarte) [2][3]

- **Stories, not just information.** Both TED ("thou shalt tell a story") and the Heath brothers' *Made to Stick* put narrative at the center of memorability. Stories are one of the six "sticky" qualities. (TED Commandments; Heath) [10][11]

- **Ruthless simplicity / high signal-to-noise.** Garr Reynolds (*Presentation Zen*) borrows the **signal-to-noise ratio** from engineering: maximize relevant elements, strip the irrelevant. Simplicity for him means clarity, essentialness, minimalism — achieved by "careful reduction of the non-essential." (Reynolds) [5][6]

- **Slides support the speaker; they are not the script.** Reynolds advocates highly visual slides (often a full-bleed image and a single line of text) that *support* rather than *narrate*. TED: "if your audience is reading, they are not listening." (Reynolds; TED) [6][1]

- **Emotional connection and credibility.** *Made to Stick*'s SUCCESs model: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories — the qualities shared by ideas that survive and spread. (Heath) [11]

- **Vulnerability and curiosity over ego/selling.** TED Commandments explicitly: reveal curiosity and passion, be vulnerable, speak of failure as well as success — and "thou shalt not sell from the stage." (TED Commandments) [10]

- **Design that reduces, not adds, mental work.** Mayer's multimedia research shows comprehension improves when you minimize irrelevant words/images/sounds (coherence) and cue the key structure (signaling). Good decks are engineered to lower cognitive load. (Mayer) [4][7]

## 2. Structure & narrative

- **Start from the Big Idea / throughline.** Decide the one thing before you build a single slide. (TED; Duarte) [1][9]
- **Use a story arc with contrast.** Duarte's Sparkline alternates "what is" vs. "what could be," ending on a "new bliss" / call to action — a rhythm she likens to the hero's journey. Every piece of content can be sorted into "today" or "tomorrow." (Duarte) [2][3]
- **Three-act framing for business communication.** Duarte maps presentations onto a 3-act structure (setup, turn, resolution) rather than a flat list of points. (Duarte) [3]
- **One slide = one idea.** TED: "No slide should support more than one point." Prefer several simple slides over one dense one — each with a single idea, image, or data point. (TED) [1]
- **Signpost the structure.** Mayer's signaling principle: explicit cues that highlight key information and its organization improve comprehension. Tell people where they are. (Mayer) [4]
- **Opening:** earn attention with something new, curious, or unexpected (TED's "wondrous new thing"; Heath's "Unexpected"). [10][11]
- **Closing:** land the single repeatable idea and the stakes (Big Idea), not a "any questions?" fade-out. (Duarte; TED) [9][1]

## 3. Visual & design principles

- **Maximize signal-to-noise.** Every element should earn its place; delete decoration. (Reynolds) [5]
- **Whitespace is a feature.** Reynolds advocates lots of empty space to spotlight the one or few important elements. Don't fill the canvas because it's there. (Reynolds) [6]
- **Big, legible type; sans-serif for projection.** TED: text must be legible to the person farthest from the stage; prefer a sans-serif (Helvetica, Verdana) over serif. (TED) [1]
- **High-resolution images, used full and purposeful.** TED: use high-res pictures/graphics; Reynolds: large illustrations, often full-page bleeds. Avoid low-res clip-art decoration. (TED; Reynolds) [1][6]
- **Data viz: clarity over complexity.** TED: "keep graphs visually clear, even if the content is complex." Tufte's broader work argues for high-resolution, information-rich displays and against chartjunk and decoration that obscures data. (TED; Tufte) [1][8]
- **Spatial contiguity:** put related words and images close together; don't make the eye hunt. (Mayer) [4]
- **Consistency:** a consistent visual system (type scale, color, layout) is itself a noise-reduction tool — irregularity reads as noise. (implied by Reynolds' SNR framing) [5]
- **Slides are support, not a teleprompter.** This is the through-line of Reynolds, TED, and Tufte alike. [6][1][8]

## 4. Delivery & cognitive load

This is where the research is strongest and most counterintuitive — and it directly contradicts the
default "bullet points + read them aloud" habit.

- **The redundancy effect is real and well-studied.** Mayer: people learn *better* from graphics + narration than from graphics + narration + identical on-screen text. Learners given redundant on-screen text scored *worse* on retention and transfer than those given none. Putting your spoken words on the slide actively hurts. (Mayer) [4][7]
- **Split attention.** When words and images are separated (in space or time), the audience must mentally juggle the two; integrate them and synchronize them (spatial + temporal contiguity). (Mayer) [4]
- **"Reading = not listening."** TED states the practical version directly: dense text forces the audience to read instead of listen, and you lose them. (TED) [1]
- **Coherence:** cut anything not serving the point — "interesting" tangents, decorative sounds/animation — because they compete for limited working memory. (Mayer) [4][7]
- **Segmenting / pacing:** break content into digestible chunks rather than one firehose. Multiple simple slides beat one complex slide partly for this reason. (Mayer; TED) [4][1]
- **Tufte's structural critique:** PowerPoint's low-resolution, bullet-driven default fragments narrative, can't express logical relationships well, and dilutes thought. His case study is the NASA *Columbia* engineering slides, where a bulleted slide buried the severity of the foam-strike risk. Reasonable people debate how far to take Tufte, but the *Columbia* example is his concrete evidence that format can corrupt reasoning. (Tufte) [8]

## 5. PLEASE DON'TS — failure-mode checklist

Each item has a one-line reason it fails. Use this as a pre-flight check.

- [ ] **Wall-of-text slides** — forces reading over listening; triggers the redundancy effect (worse retention). [4][1]
- [ ] **Reading your slides verbatim** — redundant spoken+written identical text measurably lowers comprehension. [4]
- [ ] **Headline + bullet-list as the default slide** — TED says eliminate these; Tufte says bullets fragment narrative and hide logical relationships. [1][8]
- [ ] **More than ~6 lines of text on a slide** — exceeds TED's stated max; usually one or two lines is enough. [1]
- [ ] **One dense, complex slide instead of several simple ones** — violates one-idea-per-slide; raises cognitive load. [1][4]
- [ ] **Tiny / unreadable fonts** — must be legible to the farthest seat; if they can't read it, cut it or split it. [1]
- [ ] **Decorative clip-art and low-res images** — pure noise; lowers signal-to-noise ratio. [5][6]
- [ ] **Chartjunk / 3D pie charts / decorated graphs** — decoration obscures the data; keep graphs visually clear. [8][1]
- [ ] **Irrelevant "interesting" tangents, sound effects, gratuitous animation** — violates the coherence principle; competes for working memory. [4][7]
- [ ] **Fancy/overused slide transitions** — reads as amateur, distracts from the story, makes everyone mad. Keep transitions invisible. [4] [House rule]
- [ ] **Separating labels from the visual they describe** — split-attention; integrate words and images spatially. [4]
- [ ] **No single takeaway / no throughline** — audience can't repeat one idea, so nothing sticks. [1][9]
- [ ] **Burying the stakes** — a Big Idea without "what's at stake" doesn't motivate action. [9]
- [ ] **Flat list of facts with no tension/contrast** — no "what is → what could be," so no emotional pull. [2][3]
- [ ] **Selling from the stage / ego-forward delivery** — TED Commandment violation; erodes trust. [10]
- [ ] **Cramming everything into the slide because the canvas is there** — ignores whitespace as a tool; lowers signal. [6][5]
- [ ] **Treating slides as a teleprompter** — slides should support the speaker, not be the script. [6][1][8]

## Myths flagged (adversarial check)

- **"We remember 10% of what we read, 20% of what we hear, 30% of what we see…"** — **MYTH.** These percentages are not from Edgar Dale; Dale's Cone of Experience never carried numbers. The figures were attached later (a version traces to a 1967 Mobil Oil employee piece) and have no research support. Work-Learning Research's review is emphatic: "there is no body of research that supports the data… The retention chart cannot be supported in terms of scientific validity or logical interpretability." The suspiciously round numbers are a tell. Do **not** cite these. [12][13]

- **The "10/20/30 rule" (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30pt font).** — **REAL but opinion, not research.** It's a genuine, useful heuristic coined by Guy Kawasaki (~2005, *The Art of the Start*), born from VC pitch decks. It is one experienced practitioner's rule of thumb, not an empirical finding — treat it as a sensible default for pitches, not a law. The "30pt minimum" overlaps well with TED's legibility advice; the "10 slides / 20 minutes" is context-specific to investor pitches. [14]

- **"PowerPoint makes you dumber / kills attention" as a blanket claim** — **Overstated.** Tufte's critique is specifically about the *bulleted, low-resolution default cognitive style*, not the existence of slides. Well-designed visual slides (Reynolds, TED) are endorsed by the same literature. The nuance: format defaults corrupt reasoning; slides per se don't. [8]

- **TED Commandments** are widely reproduced and consistent across sources; the canonical plaque text is paraphrased from secondary reproductions, not a TED-hosted original.
